Not to Mention Camels: the structure

The repetitions, the itinerary, the cast, the threads and the doctrine, drawn from the text of the first edition

The weighing room

The title phrase is used of a weighing. In the morning lecture Mary Morey asks for “the catch that weighs on the other side of the balance” (p. 18), and Pilgrim Dusmano answers, “It weighs so lightly that we will not even mention it” (p. 18). The thing not mentioned is the catch, Prime World, given its name “later that morning” (p. 20); the epigraph of Chapter Three ends “not to mention many camels” (p. 29). A death is weighed three times in the book. The doctors of Chapter One are gathered “to weigh his death” (p. 1); James Morey says of Pilgrim at the cult meeting, “He’ll not be weighed in life; but I believe that once somewhere we did weigh him in death” (p. 107); and the doctors of Chapter Fifteen are gathered “to weigh his death” (p. 210). The first and third weighings are the same paragraph. The slider moves it from the one page to the other; the words in red are the words that change.

p. 1 · Chapter Onep. 210 · Chapter Fifteen

The first and last chapters of the novel are one scene with the names changed: three doctors, a coroner, a brigadier, a death to be weighed, and “For about three seconds” (p. 9) at the one end and “For about three seconds” (p. 215) at the other. The page below shows how much of the book is built this way: nineteen passages of seven words or more that recur word for word across chapters, the itinerary they lie along, the cast given by the substitutions, the threads that run through the whole, and the outcome of the doctrine.

Written somewhere

The last chapter also cites the first two. Its speakers say that what they do is written somewhere, and the sentences they refer to stand in Chapters One and Two. Bogus: “It is written somewhere that I will stomp on his fingers as they weaken on his last handhold. I wonder just where it is that all these fateful things are written” (p. 213). It is written in Chapter One, by the brigadier of police: “Stomp on his damned fingers!” (p. 8). Og: “it is written somewhere that I would go to hell for him, or with him” (p. 213); written in Chapter Two, in God’s order to Noah: “If he goes to hell for a final place, you will go there too” (p. 27). Bogus on the sister and brother: “I suppose there is some fateful thing written about their following him forever” (p. 213); written in Chapter Two, in Mary’s words: “I am with him all the way, to the grave and beyond, to an endless series of graves” (p. 18).

The offices differ at the two ends. In Chapter One “The powerful friend and the powerful enemy of Tisman were not in evidence” (p. 12). In Chapter Fifteen the friend and the enemy fill the neutral offices: “Og was filling the office of coroner” (p. 210) and “Bogus was filling the office of brigadier of police” (p. 211). The last chapter also adds a proxy, “a ‘light companion’ in death, the good man Longram who had been butchered by the mobs to give some substance of a spectacle” (pp. 210–211), so that Vonk’s “That he shall die with discomfort is part of the requirement” (p. 211) is carried by another man. In Chapter One the offices are held by officials and the friend and the enemy are absent; in Chapter Fifteen the friend and the enemy hold the offices.

The arcs

“Any action in any world will inevitably set up parallel actions in parallel worlds” (p. 30; recalled p. 175) Dunlunk’s Fifth Law

The baseline is the length of the book, divided into fifteen chapters in proportion to their page counts, with page ticks at tens. Each arc above it joins two instances of a passage of seven words or more that recurs word for word in different chapters: nineteen such blocks, forty-three instances, 1,310 words, about two per cent of the novel’s 67,272. Stroke width follows the length of the block. The dotted arcs are near-repetitions below that threshold, split, inverted, or paraphrased. The arcs under the second line are formulae of five words or more that occur three times or more, and phrases searched for by name (one powerful friend and one powerful enemy, the hypnotic man, it is written somewhere, and others) that occur two to twelve times on more than one page. Hover for the first words and the pages; click, or press Enter, to open the alignment. The three layers can be shown or hidden.

Chapters One and Fifteen share three blocks; Chapters Eight and Twelve share three blocks; Chapters Eight and Eleven share two blocks; Chapters Thirteen and Fifteen share two blocks. The chapters holding the most repeated words are Twelve (251), Eight (239), Three (219). Chapter Nine, the Narrow Corner, is joined to the rest by a single seven-word phrase (p. 129).

The cast as read

Jumper
Pilger Tisman (p. 1); Pilgrim Dusmano (p. 15); the pan-morph, fixed and named Polder (p. 131); Pelion Tuscamondo and Palgrave Tacoman among twelve names listed (p. 146); Polder Dossman (p. 159).
Surety
None in evidence at the first death (p. 12). Noah Zontik, to himself: “It is hard to hold an umbrella over so protean a man as Pilgrim Dusmano” (p. 28). Lorica, to Palgrave: “I am commanded to effect it that you be a good man” (p. 148). Og Scath: “I must hold the shield over him, the schirm, the breastplate, the lorica, the umbrella” (p. 168); at the end, “Perhaps I can be the one powerful friend” (p. 212).
Good enemy
Cyrus Evenhand (p. 25), in Pilgrim’s words to Noah: “Evenhand is my one powerful enemy on this world, just as you are my one powerful friend” (p. 86). On the last world the role is divided: Bogus is the enemy and not good, “We are called Bogus because we are bogus Lords” (p. 188), and Longram is good and not an enemy but a “proxy” (p. 213).
Mock enemy
Trenchant to Bogus. Polder makes the identification and Bogus denies it. Polder: “Your name was Trenchant, and you were a false Lord Spiritual” (p. 184); Bogus: “he isn’t and he certainly wasn’t an exact correspondence to me. He hadn’t learned cruelty yet. Or disbelief” (p. 185).
Sister and brother
Maria and Jacob del Mar (p. 12); “Mary in the sunlight, James in the shadows” (p. 60); “an ariel and a dog” (p. 122) at the Corner and on Oraioi Polloi (p. 149); Moira and Jake Mara, Moira to Polder: “We don’t really want to do this, but we are compelled to” (p. 162); Doctor August at the end: “a hound and an ariel waiting outside” (p. 213).
Three doctors
Funk, Austin, and Jude Ravel (pp. 1–2); Judas Raffels and John Augustine (p. 103); Vonk, August, and Judah Raphaelson (pp. 210–211). The Jude, Judas, Judah chain runs through the three triads.
Good man killed for the spectacle
Evenhand, on whom, Raffels reports, Pilgrim “has just dined” (p. 108); Lazarus, “that good man” (p. 141); Longram, “butchered by the mobs to give some substance of a spectacle” (p. 211).
Gaps
Chapter One has no mock enemy, no good man killed, and no child. The Nine Worlds have no doctors and no enemy. On the last world the enemy’s role is divided between Bogus and Longram.

A caveat applies to both columns. The identity of the jumper across worlds is the cult figure’s doctrine, “Every parallel of me is truly myself” (p. 77), and not the narrator’s: “Are all of each set the same person?” (p. 146) is followed by “We cannot discuss here just what a person does consist of” (p. 146), and Polder himself says that “those dimensions of me on Nine Worlds might as well be dimensions of someone else” (p. 208). The table records substitution, for which the text is the evidence. It does not record identity, which is the cult figure’s claim, qualified by the narrator and by Polder.

The itinerary

Six stations, the jumper’s path in red, and six threads under it with a dot at each cited page and the page number under the dot; hover or focus a dot for the quotation. On the last thread the filled dots are the narrator’s you and the hollow dots its other asides. Chapter One is set “about fifteen years before our subsequent action” (p. 12) on a world that is never named, with “no proved correlation between the different worlds” (p. 12). Pilgrim’s world is “Present World” (p. 55); the phrase “world of record” (p. 171) belongs to Polder’s world, which Bogus calls “a boondocks world” (p. 213). The Nine Lives are drawn detached from the time line: “There is no such thing as ‘at the same time’ on Cannes World” (p. 140); they are “still going on, in a sundered-off place that nobody can visit twice” (p. 158); “You are present nine times in Nine Worlds” (p. 208). Their place between Chapters Nine and Twelve is the narration’s order of focus, “Now the focus is on Oraioi Polloi” (p. 146), and not the jumper’s order of life.

The day of Chapters Two to Eight contains the decision to die. In the morning lecture, “I’ll go on with it now for another ten years” (p. 17); at the midday dinner, to Fairfronter, “it is final that I leave tonight” (p. 75); between them the change of eyes, “Waking with new eyes was almost the same thing as waking in a new world” (p. 47). The lecture’s rule, “It’s better to have your dying under your own control” (p. 17), is kept within the day. In Chapter Nine the pan-morph is fixed in one form and given a name. The knacker: “This is your last change, and your new name is ‘Change-No-More’” (p. 126); the narrator: “But one must give up something to get a name” (p. 131). Injuries taken there are kept across the jump, and memories are not: the dog’s “smashed its skull, which scrambled its wits forever” (p. 128) is Jake’s “Someone had scrambled his brains forever.” (p. 161), and the ariel “with the wings torn clear off her” (p. 129) is Moira, whose wings were lost “somewhere, possibly at ‘the Camel’s Eye’” (p. 160).

The narrator’s second person, the literal you outside speech and epigraphs, occurs twelve times on nine pages: twice in Chapter Four, during the knifing (pp. 45, 46); twice in Chapter Nine (pp. 125, 127); three times in Chapter Ten (p. 137 and twice on p. 140); three times in Chapter Eleven, all on p. 146; once in Chapter Twelve (p. 176); and once in Chapter Fourteen, in a maxim of Polder’s given in the narrator’s voice (p. 202). Seven of the twelve fall in Chapters Nine to Eleven, where the jumper is dead or outside time, and none falls in Chapters One to Three, Five to Eight, Thirteen, or Fifteen. The narrator addresses the reader in other forms as well, marked on the thread by hollow dots: the question put and answered in a word, “But did he fall to his destruction? No, not at all.” (p. 132) and six more (pp. 3, 10, 157, 199, 200, 206); the interruption, “Wait! There was another” (p. 12) and “Wait a minute; he’s speaking now.” (p. 211); the stage direction, “(A nonessential change in worlds and lives and persons happens here.)” (p. 144); the inclusive first person, “all of us have to live on all these worlds sometime” (p. 146); and the narrator’s name for itself in the Nine Worlds: “A particular scanner may leave him there and focus on someone else in some elsewhere” (p. 145).

The children

In both halves of the book a child brings on the fall. The Pym child names the jewel eyes first, “You have jeweled eyes like a fly that’s as big as a man” (p. 54), makes the threat, “I want to stand on that narrow ledge and knock you off into hell the next time you come by” (p. 54), and keeps it at the Narrow Corner (p. 126). The Camel Town children call “Paper camel!” (p. 169), and “Polder caught and killed one of the children” (p. 170); the hecklers come, Jake reports, because “you have killed the children of some of them” (p. 202). The post boy is drained to change the scene on the horn, at Palgrave’s order, “Be drained, then” (p. 151), and the changed scene is the death Palgrave then dies (p. 156).

The motifs

One row per family, fifteen cells per row, the fill proportional to the rate per thousand words within the row, the count printed in the cell. Pleasure and fun are separate rows.

The memory family (98 instances) is heaviest by rate in Chapter Four and nearly stops after Chapter Twelve (2, 1, and 0 in the last three chapters); thirst has no instance in Chapters Fourteen and Fifteen; the surety family has none in Chapter One; the eidolon family is absent until Chapter Five and peaks in Chapter Thirteen; hell rises to its peak rate in Chapter Fifteen; the camel peaks in Chapter Twelve, the chapter of the camel catalogue. Pleasure is concentrated in the first world (49 of its 58 instances in Chapters Three to Seven, peak in Chapter Five) and fun in the last (9 of 14 in Chapters Thirteen to Fifteen); the laughing family peaks in Chapter Thirteen. On the first world the word is pleasure, and James Morey questions it: “But does he have any fun out of his enjoyments and pleasures?” (p. 62). On the last world the word is fun, and Bogus is its speaker: “It is fun to manufacture a small person” (p. 182); “it’s only fun to destroy a good person” (p. 195); “a little last fun” (p. 214). The jumper “was never a good laugher” (p. 173). The title of laughing God is promoted for him by Moira, who works “to create Polder’s image as that of the ‘laughing God’” (p. 173), and at his own order, “I’ve been having my cult promote the title the ‘Laughing God’ for myself” (p. 189); the laughter of God itself is asked for by Bogus, in the prayer “Let me walk in the light and hear the laughter of God” (p. 189).

Two questions are put in the text and decided by no one in it, and one of the rules above bypasses both. Whether Polder is a made thing or a man is “something that will have to be argued out between the eidetics and the reals” (p. 192). Whether the last world is Prime World is given only as odds, by Og, “the odds are billions to one against this being Prime World” (p. 207), and by Bogus, “But the odds are billions to one against its being Prime World” (p. 213), the odds Polder had given against hell on his arrival, “The odds are billions to one against my ever hitting on that one” (p. 159). The rule that bypasses both is that the place is decided by the mind at death, “He thought this was Prime World.” (p. 213) “Then for him it was” (p. 213), and the law of Prime World operates wherever he is.

The four last things

The set is named once, in Noah’s account of Prime World, which “is the base and anchor of the four final or eschatological things. Thankfully, there are no final things attached to any of the other worlds” (p. 21). The doctrine of Chapter Two denies all four everywhere but on Prime World, and all four arrive, under the doctrine’s own rules. Doctor Raffels announces the arrival at the cult supper: “All the final things are happening now” (p. 108). The table gives, for each of the four, the doctrine’s teaching, the substitute put in its place, and the outcome.

The end: “Then he was heard in the very deep distance, far under the queasy feet of all of them” (p. 215); “It couldn’t, of course, be an actual soul screaming forever in hell. It had to be some sort of imitation. But it was quite a good imitation” (p. 215).

The one command from heaven in the book is on the invisible sign over Polder’s head, read by Og: “Protect this least of mine” (p. 168). The words are those of the judgment in Matthew 25, unto one of the least of these my brethren. The Hand from Heaven that is not a sky-sign points at the man about to be damned and commissions his surety; at the end the surety is told that he “will only follow him down into a shallow and dingy ditch” (p. 213).

The camels

A search of all 215 pages of the first edition for camel and its compounds (camelous, humpback, humpbacked, Camel Town, Camel’s Eye) finds 105 occurrences on 34 pages. Each was read in its context. The inventory is given first, then the senses in which the camel is used.

Where the camels are

Chapter Two, 1 (p. 24). Chapter Three, 1 (p. 29, the epigraph). Chapter Six, 13 (pp. 72, 75, 79, 81, 85). Chapter Eight, 4 (pp. 108, 110, 111). Chapter Nine, 7 (pp. 121, 124). Chapter Ten, 2 (p. 144). Chapter Eleven, 3 (p. 154). Chapter Twelve, 61 (pp. 158–170, 174), of which 44 are in the catalogue of pp. 164–167. Chapter Thirteen, 1 (p. 192). Chapter Fourteen, 9 (pp. 195, 205–209). Chapter Fifteen, 3 (pp. 212, 213). Chapters One, Four, Five, and Seven have none. The first camel is a joke of Noah’s (p. 24) and the second is in the title verse, the epigraph of Chapter Three (p. 29); in Chapters Six and Eight the camel is the camel of the gnat-and-camel verse; in Chapter Nine it gives its name to the place of judgment; in the Nine Worlds it is declared the cult figure’s totem; in Chapter Twelve it is the stock of Camel Town; and the last camels are in the autopsy and in the dead hand (pp. 212, 213).

The three scriptural camels

Three scriptural texts are behind the other uses. The first is Jonah 4:11, the source of the title. The epigraph of Chapter Three reads “Nineveh, in which there are more than a hundred and twenty thousand persons that know not how to distinguish their right hand from their left, not to mention many camels” (p. 29) and is signed God. The wording follows the Douay (know not how to distinguish), not the Authorized Version (cannot discern), and the Douay’s closing words, and many beasts, are replaced by the title phrase. The verse is God’s answer to Jonah, his reason for sparing the city, and both of its members recur in the book. The people who cannot tell right from left: “The people! The people know not how to distinguish between their right hand and their left” (p. 68), Pilgrim’s sneer at the curator; on the night of the horses, “It’s being explained to the people which is their right hand and which is their left. The right and the left sometimes change places” (p. 112); and Polder’s first words on the world of record are “Which is my right hand and which is my left?” (p. 159), with the narrator’s gloss, “This confusing of the right hand and the left is a disorientation that often plagues jump-travelers” (p. 159). On arrival the jumper cannot tell his right hand from his left, like the people of the verse. The two hands are named again at the mountain: “His right hand was raised in a gesture of power. In his left hand he held an attribute, a camel goad” (p. 205).

The second text is Matthew 23:24, the gnat and the camel. The epigraph of Chapter Six: “Eclectic outrage is our rule: To strain at gnats that soil and sin us, And pass huge camels to the stool. And still there are great camels in us.” (p. 72). The narrator applies it to the Lords Spiritual, who hunt down a strong man on “a gnat’s-weight of evidence” (p. 74). “Were they Lords of the Gnats for nothing?” (p. 74); “For a people, even a good people, do not pass gnats easily, once they have gotten inside them” (p. 74); “selective passing is an oddity about even good people. They can pass out easily many very large objects, not to mention camels” (p. 75), the title phrase used a second time. Trenchant puts the verse to Pilgrim at the Media Club: “How do you justify this detailed straining at gnats, you and your sort, when you consider the great and ungainly camels that you have swallowed whole and passed through you and out again?” (p. 81); “Yes, you pass them through complete with hair and hump and hoofs, and never a difficulty at all” (p. 81); Pilgrim answers, “Cleansing for the tract, camels” (p. 81). Among the Lords present are “the Gnat Lords” (p. 81). Fairfronter, at the cult supper, puts the verse the other way about: “The microscopic good that is in Pilgrim Dusmano is the gnat reversed. Oh, how they are straining at it at this moment! Oh, how all the great Lords are straining at the stool! It’s near to destroy them (the great camel-passers, they), as can be told by their groaning” (p. 108). In Fairfronter’s account the Lords have passed the camel, Pilgrim’s evil, and are straining at the gnat, the small good in him.

The third text is Matthew 19:24, the needle’s eye. The epigraph of Chapter Nine: “It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle, than for a rich man to enter into the kingdom of heaven” (p. 121), with heaven for the verse’s God, and under it Lafferty’s own gloss from “Narrow Valley”: “You know how that camel does it? He just closes one of his own eyes and flops back his ears and plunges right through. A camel is mighty narrow when he closes one eye and flops back his ears. Besides, they use a big-eyed needle in the act” (p. 121). The place of the dead takes the name: “It was at a place sometimes called the Iron Meadows and sometimes called the Camel’s Eye; the most straitened portion of this place was known as the Narrow Corner” (p. 121). The narrator then corrects the name’s source: “This Narrow Corner was sometimes called ‘the Camel’s Eye,’ but there was either a mystery or an error in that name. The fable was of the camel and the eye of a needle. Well, but the fact was a giant camel-eye by whose favor this whole cavern existed” (p. 124). By the narrator’s account the name is from a camel’s eye and not from the needle’s eye of the verse. Of the Corner, the name is what is remembered on the next world: “The ariel had had the wings pulled off her somewhere, possibly at ‘the Camel’s Eye.’ There was a smoky memory of an encounter at a narrow place named the Camel’s Eye” (p. 160). The same verse is the edict to which the Nine Lives are the counterpart: “There is a compensation and counterpart to the ‘Eye of the Needle’ edict” (p. 133).

The camel as flesh

The common human substance is called humpbacked flesh, camelous clay, or camel flesh. Pilgrim, handed the badge cut from the dead spy’s neck: “Ah, he was one of the order of the humpbacked flesh. Do you believe, Zontik, that those of the common and camelous clay could really be avenged on a fire-flesh person like myself?” (p. 85). Fineface, naming the grossest flesh the archetype will enter: “from the fire-flame spirit-flesh of the ethereals to the gamy and humpbacked flesh of the camel (your totem animal, is it not, Pelion?)” (p. 144). Palgrave’s testament carries “its old humpback-flesh camel motif” (p. 154). Bogus, deciding to destroy Polder: “But I wonder if he may not be partly of what is called cryptically ‘humpbacked flesh,’ that plastic, less-than-human substratum that gives us so much trouble” (p. 195). The narrator, at the end of the cult: “The camel, being of primordial and humpbacked flesh, seemed to be Polder’s last creature contact” (pp. 206–207). Vonk at the autopsy: “Oh, the primordial flesh, what is sometimes called the humpbacked clay (from a passage in Rimskanski, I believe); it’s just an idea, a debasement of the old idea of a ‘vital fluid’” (p. 212), and on the slide, “eidolon fiber, human flesh, regressive or camel flesh all mixed together” (p. 212). Set these beside the two makings of man given on p. 169, “made out of the slime of the earth” (p. 169) and “made out of winged words” (p. 169), and beside Prime World, “the world of muck” (p. 21): the camel is on the side of slime and muck. The man who calls himself “a fire-flesh person” (p. 85) is found at the autopsy to be partly camel flesh (p. 212).

The camel as the people and as Prime World

Noah’s joke in Chapter Two is the first camel in the book. He is describing Prime World, the original world: “There are giants living up there, dimwit giants who are the pride of prime. This is the original world, and the dimwits are the original people. What, Pilgrim-would you swallow only half a camel? And what will you do with the rest of it?” (p. 24). In the joke, to swallow the camel is to take Prime World and its people whole; Pilgrim calls it “a malodorous rock-shrew joke” (p. 24) and leaves. Camel Town is described in the same terms. “This is Camel Town. It is the second-best town in the world in every way” (p. 163); “But Camel Town, best in nothing, is second-best in everything. It reminds me of people” (p. 164); Polder, “Camel Town is second in everything? What a humpbacked idea!” (p. 164); the lad, “Yes, like the camel. The camel is second-best at everything” (p. 164). The lad’s camel is second-best at everything; the cult figure is “a derivative” (p. 23) in Noah’s words, and preaches that “I speak of derivatives, but there are no originals ever anywhere” (p. 117); and on the world of record he comes to the second-best town.

Who sells the camels

The lad who recites the catalogue says “I sell camels” (p. 167). Polder says “Come work for me,” the lad answers “Buy my last camel and I will,” and Polder orders “Give him a camel’s worth of gold, Moira” (p. 167). Polder tells the lad “You remind me of somebody else, of an older man” (p. 167), and the lad answers “Perhaps I’ve been renovated” (p. 167); on the next page he gives his name as Oak Scath and takes the office of shield-man (p. 168). The surety of the last world is the camel seller, and the one business of Polder’s that Og agrees to promote is the camel business: “The only thing of yours that I am promoting is Amalgamated Camel Enterprises” (p. 174). The three sureties are Zontik, Lorica, and Scath. Og’s own list of what the surety holds, “the schirm, the breastplate, the lorica, the umbrella” (p. 168), includes the lorica, and zontik is the Russian word for umbrella.

Totem and attribute

The totem is declared in the Nine Worlds, “your totem animal, is it not, Pelion?” (p. 144), and entered in the register, “there was a forever-person of the camel totem” (p. 154). On the world of record the children know it at once: “How did they know that his totem animal was the camel? And what did they mean by calling him paper?” (p. 169); their last gibe names both totems, “Humpbacked Hog-Nag!” (p. 170) and “Hog and Og, they raise a fog!” (p. 170). Polder’s plan for the zodiac: “Both the camel and the boar were once in the zodiac. They were defeated in ancient feud, but they still have rights to those places. I want to become a double Lord of the Zodiac in the reconstituted signs of the camel and the boar” (p. 174). At the mountain the camel goad is the attribute in his left hand (p. 205). After the reaction, “He would ride his favorite camel out in Longram’s Acres for hours” (p. 206), the camel being his last creature contact, “But now even the camel would loft its head, peel back its upper lip, and blink in bloodshot contempt” (p. 207), and Og’s lip “curled in contempt very nearly like the camel’s” (p. 207). At the autopsy, “About all that was now left intact and unanalyzed of Polder Dossman was one hand which still held a camel’s flem for attribute, and some central masses of the still pumping heart” (p. 213). A fleam is a lancet for bleeding cattle; the attribute at the end is an instrument for drawing blood from the totem animal.

The Camel’s Revenge

A society. Its badge is found under the skin of the spy Pilgrim killed: “The token was a leather and hair badge of a group known as ‘the Camel’s Revenge.’” (p. 85). Noah: “Yes. I think they can, and I’d wish they would, if I weren’t under this foolish compulsion to protect you. Yes, it is the Camels who will kill you tonight, Pilgrim, if you’re not careful” (p. 85). Pilgrim: “I’ll trap them into killing me, Noah. Is that not clever of me? I always wanted to be done to death by mad Camels” (p. 85). They do not kill him. Fairfronter: “It will not be ‘the Camel’s Revenge’ or any such societies that have stored up resentments against him. No, it will be the Media and the Lords of the Media” (p. 108). Zontik: “He intended to trap the ‘Camels’ into murdering him tonight. What does it matter now if he is killed by the Lords instead?” (p. 110). The two bodies fight in the streets: “Certain militia of the Media Lords are battling with bravos of ‘the Camel’s Revenge.’ It’s all good clean battle, mostly for the forgotten joy of open fighting” (p. 111). Two details connect the society to the last world. Pilgrim had addressed the spy, before the badge was found, as “young camel” (p. 79); the spy was Trenchant; and Trenchant is Bogus, “Your name was Trenchant, and you were a false Lord Spiritual” (p. 184). The man who cuts the sliver from Polder’s heart and keeps it screaming belonged to the Camel’s Revenge. That the revenge Pilgrim tried to arrange on the first world is taken on the last is a reading; the identification is in the text and the conclusion is not.

The camel’s milk and the camel’s coin

Twice camel milk is the drink brought against the thirst. On arrival: “Someone brought him a curious cup that was filled with either green coconut milk or green camel milk. He drained it off noisily, and it took the screaming edge off his thirst” (p. 159). On reanimation: “Water, green camel milk, and whey were brought to Polder Dossman” (p. 192). Lazarus had to pay gold for drops; the camel’s milk is brought to Polder. The coin Moira has him flip has Cities on one face and Camels on the other: “If it comes up ‘Cities,’ then that means this is not Prime World. And if it comes up ‘Camels,’ that also means this is not Prime World” (p. 208); “The coin didn’t come down ‘Cities.’ It didn’t come down ‘Camels,’ either” (p. 209). The city and the beasts are the two things spared in the Jonah verse. That the faces of the coin are Jonah’s is a reading; the two words are given and nothing more.

The nursery camel

The epigraph of Chapter Twelve, the Mary Morey rhyme, is signed “Woolly Camel Book of Nursery Rhymes” (p. 158): a real rhyme under an invented camel book, at the head of the chapter in which Camel Town is reached.

Four senses of the camel

Four senses of the camel can be separated. First, it is the common creature: humpbacked flesh and camelous clay (p. 85), the people who cannot tell right from left (p. 29), the town that is second-best in everything (pp. 163–164). Pilgrim passes it through him, as the Lords do, and calls the passing a cleansing (p. 81). Second, it is the totem: declared on Cannes (p. 144), entered in the register (p. 154), made a business on the world of record (p. 174), and sold by Og, who promotes nothing else of Polder’s. Third, it is the eye: the name of the place where the dead are judged (p. 121), the eye by whose favor the cavern exists (p. 124), the eyeball of the catalogue that wards off the evil eye (p. 165), the eye of the gloss that closes to pass the needle (p. 121), and the eye that looks at Polder in bloodshot contempt (p. 207). Fourth, it is the beast of Jonah 4:11, the verse of the epigraph (p. 29), in which God gives the camels as part of his reason for sparing Nineveh. No later passage takes up the sparing. What the last chapters give instead is the man who set himself above the camelous clay (p. 85) found to be partly camel flesh (p. 212), holding a camel’s fleam (p. 213), with the sliver of his heart cut by Bogus, who was Trenchant of the Camel’s Revenge (pp. 85, 184, 214).

Reading and statement

Three points above go beyond what is stated. The revenge taken by Bogus, the former Trenchant, is inferred from his membership (pp. 79, 85, 184) and his act (p. 214); the word revenge is not used of the act. The faces of the coin are read as the two members of Jonah 4:11; the words Cities and Camels are given without comment. And the identity of Polder’s favorite camel (p. 206) with the last camel bought from Og (p. 167) is not stated; one camel is bought and one is ridden, and nothing joins them. The rest is stated in the text.

The logic of archetypes

Six things are said in the book about what an archetype is, and Chapter Fourteen puts them to a test. The six are set out below with their evidence, then the test, then a note of which parts of this account are reading rather than statement.

An archetype is a publication in the group unconscious

Fineface, selling Pelion the idea on Cannes, begins with the ocean: “This ocean that underlies every earth,” Fineface was saying, “that underlies every creature and manifestation, that infuses every mind and memory” (p. 142). He gives it its names, “The recording ocean is known on some of the worlds as the ‘Group Unconscious’ and on others as the ‘Folk Ocean,’” (p. 142), and then defines it as a publishing system. It “is made up of the personal testaments of a group of devils” (p. 143), known “by their literary or eidetic styles in this repository, which is the most plastic of all the mass (non-lineal, of oblated bulk of mass) media” (p. 143). An archetype on these terms is an author whose testament has “achieved group-unconscious publication” (p. 143). Distribution is total and permanent: “any trace of substance in any part of it is immediately in all parts of it” (p. 143); “When one is ‘in’ there, one is in forever” (p. 144). The wish that this serves is stated before the ocean is described: “You wish to give testimony of your personal flame and image. As a cult figure, you wish the largest possible publication for your testimony” (p. 141), and Pelion accepts the description, “I would like to publish myself in every extent of every ocean that underlies every world” (p. 141).

The number of archetypes is limited, and new ones are manufactured

The published devils are “a few more than one hundred” (p. 143). They were lettered, then written in the Tarshish Syllabary, then given the symbols of the elements: “It is believed that there will be the same number of these devils publishing their testaments as there are elements in the universes” (p. 143). New archetypes and new elements arrive together, and the seats left are the unstable ones: “the discovery of the nine most recent elements has coincided with the discovery of the nine most recent of the scrivener or media devils. Should you reach this inner circle, Pelion, your equivalent would have to be an unstable element; that’s the only kind left” (p. 143). The admission scene follows this rule. The post boy says, “They have just discovered—no, they have just manufactured a new element” (p. 152), and two pages later Palgrave understands: “that new element had to be the correspondence to himself. He knew now that he had been accepted into the unstable-hundred company of the archetypical devil-angels” (p. 154). Three things follow. The archetype is manufactured; every new one corresponds to an unstable element; and the company has a limited number of places.

A place can be bought

“May one not, by fabulous expenditure, buy a membership? May one not buy a pew-stall in this most unchurchly of churches? Yes, I think it may be done. I think it has been done” (p. 143). The price is shown in the drama played while the terms are set. Lazarus drops mortgage-gold into the cup, and “it drank up many coins for every drop of water that it brimmed over” (p. 142); the buyer of an archetype is told, “You will buy the pervading water as Lazarus does, but you will buy oceans as he buys drops” (p. 142). What Pelion buys from Fineface is access: “I can tell you who can tell you who can tell you who. You must pay heavy toll at each station, of course” (p. 144). As he pays, the cup “developed a deep crack from the weight of all the heavy gold coins placed in it, and all the water ran out of it” (p. 144). On the next world Lorica’s first words to Palgrave are “You’ll pay too much to become an archetype” (p. 147). The precedent is stated as a trade: “It has been done by several of the Media or Eidetic Lords of various worlds. It has been done by several of the cult figures on and between the worlds” (p. 144). Both the makers of images and the images themselves have bought places. The company Palgrave enters is headed by “the most select company of Simon Magus and Baubo and Demeter” (p. 154); Simon Magus is the man in Acts who offered money for the gift of the Spirit.

What is published is a totem and a portrait

What is published is a motif. On Cannes it is the camel, “your totem animal, is it not, Pelion?” (p. 144); on Oraioi Polloi “his testament, with its new bristle-boar motif, had now become a part of every pool of that ocean named the group unconscious” (p. 154); on the world of record Polder asks, “How did they know that the boar had become his secondary totem animal? How did they know that Adonis had become his secondary totem person?” (p. 169). The portrait of the fair-haired man is the published testament. It recurs, reshuffled, at pp. 37, 115, 139, and 203, and its first appearance says how such a portrait is spread: “He had been called, in the popular press, the hypnotic man, the electric man, the magnetic man, the transcendent man. Why should he not be called such things in the popular press? He paid men well to call him those things in that place. Praise is one of the primordial pleasures that can be bought for money, or for more tenuous barter. And genuine praise will often flourish after its synthetic foreshadow has delineated the way” (p. 37). Paid repetition comes first and belief after; the four tellings of the portrait follow that order. In Chapter Five the narrator says what the image is: “Oh, it was just the image of his own ideated self” (p. 59).

The archetype is a derivative, and claims to outrank the original

Three characters say so, each from his own side. Noah: “I believe that you had your origin in the compensating imagination of some particularly dull individual, some prime person indeed. You are a projection, even a secondary projection” (p. 23). Pilgrim: “Why, I’m a bundle of sticks, Fairfronter. I’m a million long and skinny sticks bundled together” (p. 77); “The sticks are my parallel persons” (p. 77); “Every parallel of me is truly myself, but we cannot see this clearly yet” (p. 77). Bogus, with the sculptor whose statue carved a better statue, “so it went till there were nine of the statues or eidolons variously made” (p. 196), until “they all turned on the old sculptor himself and began to unlimb him for his improvement” (p. 196); Jake answers for the derivatives, “How would there be improvement if the first flesh should carry veto power over its superior derivatives?” (p. 197). The children of Camel Town say the same thing in three gibes, “Paper camel! Paper camel!” (p. 169), “Hot-air camel! Hot-air camel!” (p. 169), “Paint-picture camel! Paint-picture camel!” (p. 169), and Polder’s own thought takes the three words as names of his substance: “such holy things as paper (the first and still the best of the miracle communicators), against such things as hot air (electronic ether), against such things as paint-picture or visual portrayal” (p. 169). The narration then gives the account of creation that applies to such men: “In one legendary, man had been made out of the slime of the earth. But now men, and especially transcending men, were made out of winged words, out of contrived and multidimensional portraits, out of the dripping charisma of the gifted” (p. 169).

The archetype is inside every head

The promise is “You will be able to lodge your flame and your image in every mind and every flesh” (p. 144). The narration, in Polder’s own voice, states it as done: “But had not Polder himself now become a monster (a monstrum, a showpiece) in that interior ocean? Was he not an archetypical manifestation? He was on the inside of the head of every person on every world; he was there in his own person” (p. 170). Intuitive Implementation follows from it. The abdication commands need no machinery because “The intuition of Polder’s personality was the strongest thing going out” (p. 199); he sends “twelve billions of sets of abdication commands to twelve billion worlds” (p. 199); and the edge of the reach is marked, “Here and there the messages encountered absolute incomprehension” (p. 199).

The test

If the archetype is inside every head, nothing in any head is hidden from him. Polder draws the inference himself: “I am an Archetype,” he whispered melodiously in one of his golden asides. “I am a Zodiac Lord. I am one of the High Dice Throwers, and our dicing is another name for Fate. How can anything be happening that I do not know about? How can there be meanings hidden from me?” (p. 201). Chapter Fourteen then supplies hidden meanings one after another. The crowd laughs at the tooth filled with a plug stamped “Plugs by Suggs” (p. 200), and “he still didn’t understand the laughing. Shouldn’t an Archetype and a Zodiac Lord and a High Dicer even understand his own joke?” (p. 200). The hecklers are shielded, and “Who had armed them against it, and how?” (p. 201) is a question he cannot answer; Jake has to go and fetch the reason, “The hecklers are angry because you have killed the children of some of them” (p. 202), which was set down earlier, when “Polder caught and killed one of the children” (p. 170) after he had resolved that “he would have to have some of those children killed as an example” (p. 169). Moira separates his failures from Bogus’s effects: “But some are your own devices. The failures are your own” (p. 202).

Three witnesses give three verdicts. Og: “He is genuine,” Og said. “He has become one of the archetypes in the ocean that underlies every mind. He has become one of the Lords of the Zodiac. This is greatness” (p. 197). Bogus: “it isn’t genuineness; it isn’t greatness. It’s just the way the dice roll” (p. 197). Moira: “It does not really matter whether Polder has become one of the High Seven Dice Throwers,” Moira said. “But it must be believed that he has become one of the seven. I suspect, with the treasonable part of my mind, that he has not become either Archetype or Zodiac Lord. But it must be believed that he is these things” (p. 198). None of the three is corrected by the narrator; what follows is the challenge and the autopsy. At the challenge, “Polder Dossman, eidolon-man and cult figure, Archetype and Zodiac Lord, member of the High Seven Dicers who are the same as Fate, rose to his heights” (p. 203), and the fourth telling of the portrait ends with a clause the earlier three did not have: “He was the man with the magic hands that dripped light, that dripped grace and gift, that dripped seed and solace, that dripped healing. That dripped death” (p. 203). The mountain moves, the reaction comes, and the autopsy finds the totem animal in the flesh: “eidolon fiber, human flesh, regressive or camel flesh all mixed together” (p. 212). Bogus accounts for the death as a failure of equipment: “An overload failed. It should have blown quite early in the mountain-moving caper. But the overload failed to blow, and the creature blew instead” (p. 212).

The open question and the ending

The question of the archetype’s reality is put in its general form at the reanimation: “Whether his was only a Media-created, eidetic life or whether it was a real life is something that will have to be argued out between the eidetics and the reals” (p. 192). One test is proposed, Og’s: “His life is real. Eidetic contrivances do not suffer resurrection thirst on being reanimated” (p. 192); and one rebuttal, Bogus’s: “No, of course they don’t. But sometimes they think they do. They mimic living forms in both mental and physical processes” (p. 192). The last sentences of the book have the same form, applied to the damnation: “It couldn’t, of course, be an actual soul screaming forever in hell. It had to be some sort of imitation. But it was quite a good imitation” (p. 215). They also have the form of the sentence on p. 37, a synthetic thing doing the work of the real one. And the scream is a residue of the kind described in Chapter One, “Cult figures particularly did often leave a fast-spreading residue” (p. 3). At the first death the residue was hedged, “Likely he had. And likely it was stolen, to be suppressed, by the first of the monitors who discovered it” (p. 10); at the last death it is heard by everyone.

The terms taken from Jung

The terms are Jung’s, and each is changed. Jung’s archetypes are inherited, impersonal, not for sale, and not counted; Lafferty’s are authored, personal, bought, and numbered on a table with vacancies. The change is made with a second vocabulary, that of media: “the most plastic of all the mass (non-lineal, of oblated bulk of mass) media” (p. 143), the paper, the “electronic ether” (p. 169), and the picture of p. 169, and the sky-sign on the Hand from Heaven, “Sky-Signs and Prodigies by Multi-Media Productions. Call us!” (p. 170). In the book the group unconscious is a broadcast system, and the archetype is a cult figure who has bought publication on it.

Reading and statement

Four points in this account go beyond what is stated. That Palgrave’s admission is a purchase is inferred from the sequence, the toll paid at p. 144 and the acceptance at p. 154; what is stated is that membership can be bought, “I think it has been done” (p. 143). That the tongues of fire of Chapter Fourteen, “the tongues of fire that flicked in and out of the mouth of Polder” (p. 197) and the tongue “that hovered over his head night and day” (p. 198) for each convert, are the simony delivered is a connection made through Simon Magus; his name is in the company and nothing more is said of him. That the last sentences repeat Bogus’s rebuttal is a parallel of form; there is no cross-reference. And the inference from step six to the test, that an archetype inside every head can have nothing hidden from him, is Polder’s own (p. 201); it fails in his case in Chapter Fourteen, but nothing in the narrator’s voice says that the refutation decides between the three verdicts, and Moira’s position, that it must be believed, is the one the cult acts on to the end.

The epigraphs

Twenty-one epigraphs on sixteen pages. Seven name a real source: five real authors or scriptures, Lafferty’s own story “Narrow Valley,” and a real nursery rhyme under an invented book. Fourteen are invented, and two of these, Arpad Arutinov’s history and the Enniscorthy Chronicle, belong to Lafferty’s own apparatus.
